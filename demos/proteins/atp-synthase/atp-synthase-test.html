  • You cannot see a proton. It has no electrons, so nothing here shows the ion actually driving the motor — only the glutamate each c-subunit carries it on.
  • Eight ions, three ATP — the numbers don't divide. That mismatch is why the shaft has to be springy: the ring can only stop every 45°, and the head wants steps of 120°.
  • Matched by conformation, not by chain letter. Depositors' labels travel with the rotor, so states are compared by best-fitting subunit, and the two sites are fitted on the P-loop alone so the domain swing stays visible. No view here holds real ATP — the one deposition that tried used an unbreakable stand-in, and was cut at review.

Almost all the ATP in you is made by one machine: a rotor turned by protons falling across a membrane, mechanically wringing ATP out of ADP and phosphate. It is the only enzyme most people meet that is literally a motor, and it runs at over a hundred revolutions a second.

  • Two pieces, two jobs. F₀ is a membrane ring turned by protons falling down their gradient; F₁'s α₃β₃ head sits on the γ shaft bolted to it, and each turn deforms a β subunit.
  • Boyer's binding change mechanism. The three catalytic sites are never in the same state — open, holding, clamped shut — and a third of a turn moves each one on. Making the bond costs almost nothing; releasing it is what the rotation pays for. Walker's 1994 structure confirmed it, and they shared the 1997 Nobel.
  • It runs backwards too. Starved of oxygen, the motor burns ATP to pump protons instead — which is what IF1, the violet chain on the human view, jams shut.
