# How do we know?

Textbook facts about tiny things that seem impossible to know, and the trick that cracked each one.
None of these are in [HISTORIC.md](HISTORIC.md); this doc picks up where it leaves off, and
[RESPIRATION-HISTORY.md](RESPIRATION-HISTORY.md) covers cellular respiration.

Dates and details are from memory, so check them before they go into a lesson.

**Levels:** B = bench · M = microscope · C = cell · Mol = molecule (see [MICRO.md](MICRO.md)).

---

## Inside the nucleus

| How do we know... | The trick | Sim idea | Levels |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Humans have 46 chromosomes?** | For about 30 years the textbook number was 48. In 1956 Joe Hin Tjio and Albert Levan swelled cells in dilute salt water so the chromosomes spread apart, and finally counted them cleanly. | Count a crowded spread and see why 48 seemed right; then swell the cells and count again. | M, C |
| **The genetic code is read in threes?** | Francis Crick and Sydney Brenner (1961) added or deleted DNA letters in a phage gene. One or two changes broke it; three changes close together restored it. Only a triplet code behaves that way. | A word puzzle: insert or delete letters in a sentence of three-letter words and find when it reads again. | Mol |
| **mRNA exists?** | Brenner, François Jacob and Matthew Meselson (1961) grew bacteria with heavy ribosomes, then infected them with a phage. The phage's new RNA went onto the old heavy ribosomes, so ribosomes are reusable readers of a short-lived message. | Color ribosomes heavy and light, follow the new RNA, and spin the result in a centrifuge tube. | B, Mol |
| **DNA copies one strand in short pieces?** | Reiji and Tsuneko Okazaki (1968) gave cells a radioactive label for just seconds. The newest DNA showed up as short fragments, which were later joined together. | Shorten the label pulse and watch the fragment sizes change; zoom into the replication fork. | Mol |
| **The order of letters in DNA?** | Frederick Sanger's method (1977) stops copying at each letter in turn, then sorts the pieces by length on a gel, so you read the sequence up a ladder. | Run four reactions, load the gel, and read the sequence from the bands. | B, Mol |
| **You can make millions of different antibodies?** | Susumu Tonegawa (1976) compared DNA from embryo cells and from antibody-making cells. The DNA had been cut and rearranged: cells shuffle gene pieces like a deck of cards. | Shuffle gene segments to build antibodies, then compare the two DNA samples on a gel. | C, Mol |

## Inside the cell

| How do we know... | The trick | Sim idea | Levels |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cells run on a division clock?** | Tim Hunt (1982) tracked proteins in dividing sea urchin eggs. One built up and then vanished at every division. He named it cyclin. | A protein graph that rises and crashes in time with the dividing eggs. | M, C, Mol |
| **Cells relay signals inside?** | Earl Sutherland (1950s) split liver into membranes and liquid. Adrenaline worked only if it met the membranes first: they made a small inside messenger (cAMP) that the liquid then responded to. | Mix the fractions in different orders and find which order works. | B, C, Mol |
| **Proteins carry a zip code?** | Günter Blobel (1970s) showed new proteins have a short tag at the front that steers them into the right compartment; without the tag they go astray. | Swap tags between proteins and watch where each one ends up. | C, Mol |
| **Lysosomes exist?** | Christian de Duve (1955) noticed by accident that an enzyme's activity went up after his samples sat in the fridge. Something was leaking out of small bags that were breaking open. | Store, freeze or stir samples and watch the activity rise as the bags break. | B, C |
| **Mitochondria were once bacteria?** | They have their own DNA, bacteria-like ribosomes and double membranes, and divide on their own. Lynn Margulis argued it in 1967; DNA comparisons later backed her up. | Compare a mitochondrion and a bacterium side by side, feature by feature. | C, Mol |
| **What a protein looks like?** | John Kendrew solved myoglobin in 1958 from thousands of X-ray diffraction spots. Today cryo-electron microscopy freezes molecules and averages thousands of images. | Average noisy images one at a time and watch the shape come into focus. | Mol |

## Motion and machines

| How do we know... | The trick | Sim idea | Levels |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Bacterial flagella spin like propellers?** | Michael Silverman and Melvin Simon (1974) stuck a bacterium's flagellum to a glass slide. The whole cell spun around. | Tether the flagellum and the cell starts spinning; reverse the motor. | M, C |
| **Muscles contract by sliding filaments?** | Two teams in 1954 (Hugh Huxley and Jean Hanson; Andrew Huxley and Rolf Niedergerke) measured muscle bands during contraction. The dark bands kept their length while the light bands shrank, so the filaments slide past each other. | Contract a muscle fiber and measure the bands; zoom down to sliding filaments. | M, C, Mol |
| **Motor proteins take steps?** | Laser "tweezers" held a bead on one kinesin molecule (Karel Svoboda, Steven Block and colleagues, 1993). It moved in steps of exactly 8 nm, the length of one tubulin unit. | Trap the bead, watch the position trace climb in steps. | Mol |
| **Single ion channels open and shut?** | Erwin Neher and Bert Sakmann (1976) sealed a tiny glass pipette onto a cell membrane. The current jumped in square steps as one channel opened and closed. | Seal the pipette and watch a current trace of square steps. | B, Mol |

## Light and energy

| How do we know... | The trick | Sim idea | Levels |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Water is split in four steps?** | Bessel Kok (1970) gave algae very short flashes of light in the dark. Oxygen came out mostly on every fourth flash, so the enzyme stores four charges before it splits water. | Flash the light and count the oxygen bursts; a pattern-finding puzzle. | B, Mol |
| **There are two photosystems?** | Robert Emerson (1957) found red and far-red light together drive more photosynthesis than the two separately added up. Two systems were working in series. | Mix light colors and compare the sums. | B, Mol |
| **How eyes detect light?** | George Wald (1950s) showed light straightens one bent molecule (retinal), and that this tiny shape change starts the whole signal. | Shine light on rhodopsin and watch retinal flip. | Mol |

## Life's big picture

| How do we know... | The trick | Sim idea | Levels |
|---|---|---|---|
| **There are three domains of life?** | Carl Woese (1977) compared ribosome RNA across microbes. Some "bacteria" were as different from bacteria as we are: the archaea. | Compare sequences and build the tree; watch the archaea split off. | Mol |
| **When species split apart?** | The molecular clock (Emile Zuckerkandl and Linus Pauling, 1962): mutations pile up at a roughly steady rate, so counting differences gives time. | Count differences between species and date their split. | Mol |
| **Humans have about 20,000 genes?** | The Human Genome Project (2001) counted, expecting about 100,000. We have about as many as a roundworm. | Guess, then compare gene counts across species. | Mol |

## First sims to build

The picks that made the launch and first-year lists are in [ROADMAP.md](ROADMAP.md).

1. **Kok's flashes:** flash the light and oxygen pops out every fourth time.
2. **The spinning bacterium:** tether the flagellum and the cell spins.
3. **Crick and Brenner's frameshifts:** a word puzzle where students find "three" themselves.
4. **Counting chromosomes:** see why 48 seemed right, then do the swelling trick.
5. **Hunt's cyclin:** a protein graph that rises and crashes with each division.
6. **The patch clamp:** square steps of current from one channel.

**New props:** laser trap with bead, patch pipette and current trace, flash lamp with oxygen
electrode, density-gradient tube (shared with Meselson–Stahl), sequencing gel. Everything else is in
the other catalogs.
