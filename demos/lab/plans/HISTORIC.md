# Landmark experiments to recreate

Famous experiments that changed biology, and what a sim of each could show. This goes with
[MICRO.md](MICRO.md) (labs that zoom in) and the prop catalogs.

Dates and details are from memory, so check them before they go into a lesson.

**Storylines:** [RESPIRATION-HISTORY.md](RESPIRATION-HISTORY.md) follows how glycolysis and cellular
respiration were figured out, from Lavoisier to the spinning ATP synthase.
[HOW-DO-WE-KNOW.md](HOW-DO-WE-KNOW.md) covers more "how did we figure that out?" facts, such as
46 chromosomes, triplet codons and spinning flagella.

**Why these work as sims:** most of these experiments were clever ways of inferring something
invisible from a visible result. The sim can show both: the bench setup the scientist actually saw, and
the cell or molecule level they had to imagine. Many also used equipment, organisms or radioactive
labels that a school can't use, so a sim is the only way students get to "run" them.

**Levels:** B = bench · M = microscope · C = cell · Mol = molecule (see MICRO.md).
**Rating:** ★★★ = strong visual story, and the zoom explains the result · ★★ = good · ★ = mostly a
bench story.

## 1. Plants and photosynthesis: where does a plant's mass come from?

| Experiment | Who, when | On the bench | What the sim adds | Levels | Rating |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **The mass of a tree** | Jan Baptist van Helmont, about 1640s | A 5 lb willow planted in 200 lb of dried soil, given only water for 5 years. The tree reached about 169 lb; the soil lost about 2 oz. He concluded the mass came from water. | Fast-forward 5 years with a scale under the pot. Then the twist he missed: zoom into a leaf and watch CO₂ from the air become sugar and wood. Most of the mass is carbon from the air. | B, C, Mol | ★★★ |
| **Mint revives the air** | Joseph Priestley, 1771–72 | A candle under a sealed jar goes out. A sprig of mint in the jar for some days "restores" the air so a candle burns again. (He also used mice; show those abstractly or leave them out.) | Gas molecules in the jar: the flame uses up O₂ and adds CO₂; the plant reverses it. A live O₂ / CO₂ meter. | B, Mol | ★★★ |
| **Light is needed** | Jan Ingenhousz, 1779 | Leaves underwater give off bubbles in sunlight, not in the dark. Only green parts do it. | Toggle light and watch bubbles start and stop; zoom into chloroplasts making O₂. | B, C | ★★ |
| **Which colors drive photosynthesis** | Theodor Engelmann, 1882 | Shone a prism's spectrum along a strand of the alga Spirogyra, with oxygen-loving bacteria in the water. The bacteria crowded at the red and blue parts. | One of the best microscope stories there is: a rainbow across the alga, and bacteria swimming to where the O₂ is. Students can move the spectrum or change the light. | M, C | ★★★ |
| **The oxygen comes from water** | Ruben, Kamen and colleagues, 1941 | Gave algae water labeled with heavy oxygen (¹⁸O). The heavy oxygen came out as O₂ gas. | Color the oxygen atoms by source and follow them: from water to O₂, from CO₂ to sugar. Students predict first. | Mol | ★★★ |
| **The Calvin cycle** | Melvin Calvin, Andrew Benson, James Bassham, about 1950 | Fed algae radioactive CO₂ in a flat "lollipop" flask, killed samples after seconds, and separated the compounds on paper chromatograms. | Radioactive carbon moving through the cycle step by step; the chromatogram's spots appearing in order as the time between labeling and killing grows. | B, Mol | ★★ |
| **Plants bend toward light** | Charles and Francis Darwin, 1880 | Covered or cut the tips of grass seedlings. Only seedlings with uncovered tips bent toward light. | Time-lapse of the seedlings bending; zoom into the shaded side, where cells stretch more. | B, C | ★★ |
| **Auxin in agar blocks** | Frits Went, 1926–28 | Put cut seedling tips on agar blocks, then set the blocks off-center on topless seedlings. The seedlings bent away from the block's side, with no light at all. | Hormone molecules diffusing from the tip into the agar and down one side of the stem. | B, C, Mol | ★★ |
| **Transpiration** | Stephen Hales, 1727 | Measured water a plant drew up and gave off through its leaves; root pressure in a tall glass tube. | Water molecules pulled up through the xylem as leaves evaporate water. | B, C, Mol | ★★ |

## 2. Cells and microbes

| Experiment | Who, when | On the bench | What the sim adds | Levels | Rating |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Cork "cells"** | Robert Hooke, 1665 | Looked at thin slices of cork through his compound microscope and drew rows of little boxes. | Use a model of his microscope with its blurry optics, then compare with a modern view of what he was seeing (dead cell walls). | B, M | ★★ |
| **"Animalcules"** | Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, 1670s | Saw tiny swimming creatures in pond water and tooth scrapings through single-lens microscopes he made himself. | Hold the tiny microscope up to the light; protists and bacteria swimming. Compare his descriptions with what we know now. | B, M | ★★★ |
| **Maggots don't come from meat** | Francesco Redi, 1668 | Jars of meat: open, sealed, and covered with gauze. Maggots appeared only where flies could lay eggs. | Time-lapse of each jar; flies landing; zoom in on eggs on the gauze. | B | ★★ |
| **The swan-neck flask** | Louis Pasteur, 1859 | Boiled broth in flasks with long S-shaped necks. It stayed clear for years; tilting it so broth touched the dust in the neck made it cloudy. | Dust and microbes trapped in the bend of the neck; tilt the flask yourself and watch the broth go cloudy. | B, M | ★★★ |
| **Germs cause disease** | Robert Koch, 1876–82 | Showed that one microbe (anthrax, then TB) caused one disease; grew bacteria on solid plates so each colony was one kind. | Streak plating: one cell becomes one colony. Show the logic of his postulates as a step-by-step puzzle. | B, M, C | ★★ |
| **Gram stain** | Hans Christian Gram, 1884 | A staining recipe that splits bacteria into purple and pink. | The same story as the Gram-stain lab in MICRO.md: wall thickness decides whether the dye stays. | M, C | ★★★ |
| **Penicillin** | Alexander Fleming, 1928 | Noticed a mold on a staph plate with a clear zone around it where bacteria didn't grow. | The contaminated plate; zoom into the zone where penicillin stops bacteria from building walls and they burst. | B, M, C, Mol | ★★★ |
| **The membrane is two layers** | Evert Gorter and François Grendel, 1925 | Spread the lipids from red blood cells into a single layer on water. It covered about twice the cells' surface area. | Spread the lipids yourself and measure; then fold the layer into a bilayer around a cell. | B, Mol | ★★ |
| **Membranes are fluid** | Larry Frye and Michael Edidin, 1970 | Fused a mouse cell and a human cell, with their surface proteins tagged in two colors. Within about 40 minutes the colors were fully mixed. | The two colors mixing across the fused cell; drop the temperature and the mixing stops. | M, C, Mol | ★★★ |
| **The nucleus controls the cell** | Joachim Hämmerling, 1930s–40s | Grafted pieces of two species of the giant single-celled alga Acetabularia. The cap that regrew matched the species of the nucleus. | Cut and graft the "stalk" and "foot" yourself; watch the new cap grow. | B, C | ★★★ |
| **The path of a secreted protein** | George Palade and colleagues, 1960s | Pulse-chase: fed cells a brief dose of radioactive amino acids, then followed where the label went over time. | Label moving from ER to Golgi to vesicles to the outside. A natural "follow the molecule" lesson. | C | ★★★ |
| **Cell-free fermentation** | Eduard Buchner, 1897 | Ground yeast with sand, pressed out the juice, and showed it still turned sugar into alcohol with no living cells. | Grind the yeast; show the enzymes still working outside the cells. | B, Mol | ★★ |

## 3. Heredity and DNA

| Experiment | Who, when | On the bench | What the sim adds | Levels | Rating |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Pea crosses** | Gregor Mendel, 1856–63 | Crossed pea plants for traits like seed shape and color and counted thousands of offspring: 3:1 ratios. | Do the crosses with a garden of plants; then show the alleles sorting into egg and pollen cells. | B, C | ★★★ |
| **Sex-linked genes** | Thomas Hunt Morgan, 1910 | Found a white-eyed male fruit fly; the trait followed the X chromosome through crosses. | Breed flies; follow the X chromosome through the generations. | B, M, C | ★★ |
| **Jumping genes** | Barbara McClintock, 1940s–50s | Explained spotted corn kernels by pieces of DNA moving around the genome. | A kernel's spots; zoom into a gene being disrupted and restored in one cell lineage. | B, C, Mol | ★★ |
| **The transforming principle** | Frederick Griffith, 1928 | Harmless "rough" bacteria became deadly "smooth" ones after mixing with heat-killed smooth bacteria. (He used mice; show the result abstractly or with colony types.) | DNA from dead cells taken up by living ones, which change type. Ties to our Ecoli transformation lab. | B, C, Mol | ★★★ |
| **DNA is the genetic material** | Oswald Avery, Colin MacLeod, Maclyn McCarty, 1944 | Repeated Griffith with extracts treated with enzymes that destroy protein, RNA or DNA. Only destroying DNA stopped transformation. | A "remove one ingredient" puzzle; the student picks an enzyme and sees which kind of molecule it chops. | B, Mol | ★★★ |
| **The blender experiment** | Alfred Hershey and Martha Chase, 1952 | Grew phages with radioactive sulfur (protein) or phosphorus (DNA), let them infect bacteria, then knocked the phage coats off in a kitchen blender. The DNA label went into the cells. | Phages landing and injecting DNA; the blender shearing off empty coats; a centrifuge separating the two. A great mix of bench and molecule. | B, C, Mol | ★★★ |
| **Chargaff's rules** | Erwin Chargaff, about 1950 | Measured bases in DNA from many species: A always equals T, and G equals C. | Count bases in DNA from different species; then show the pairing that explains it. | Mol | ★★ |
| **Photo 51** | Rosalind Franklin and Raymond Gosling, 1952 | An X-ray diffraction photo of DNA with an X-shaped pattern that signals a helix. | Shine X-rays through a DNA fiber; turn the helix and watch the pattern change. Explains how a pattern implies a shape. | B, Mol | ★★★ |
| **Building the double helix** | James Watson and Francis Crick, 1953 | Built physical models from metal plates and rods using Franklin's data and Chargaff's rules. | A model-building puzzle: fit the bases until the pairs are all the same width. | Mol | ★★ |
| **DNA copies itself semi-conservatively** | Matthew Meselson and Franklin Stahl, 1958 | Grew bacteria on heavy nitrogen, moved them to light nitrogen, and spun their DNA in a density gradient. The bands showed each new helix keeps one old strand. | Often called the most beautiful experiment in biology: color old and new strands, run generations, watch the bands in the centrifuge tube shift. Compare with the models that were ruled out. | B, Mol | ★★★ |
| **Mutations are random** | Salvador Luria and Max Delbrück, 1943 | The fluctuation test: counts of phage-resistant colonies varied wildly between cultures, which fit mutations arising at random before any exposure. | Run many cultures and see the jackpots; show mutations happening before the phage arrives. | B, C | ★★ |
| **Replica plating** | Joshua and Esther Lederberg, 1952 | Pressed velvet on a plate and stamped the colony pattern onto plates with antibiotic or phage. Resistant colonies were already there before exposure. | Stamp the velvet yourself; match colonies between plates. | B, M | ★★★ |
| **One gene, one enzyme** | George Beadle and Edward Tatum, 1941 | Mutated bread mold with X-rays; each mutant needed one added nutrient, so each gene made one enzyme. | Mutants on plates with different nutrients; a broken step in a metabolic pathway. | B, Mol | ★★ |
| **Cracking the genetic code** | Marshall Nirenberg and Heinrich Matthaei, 1961 | Added a synthetic RNA made only of U to a cell-free system. It made a protein made only of phenylalanine. | Feed RNA strings to a ribosome and see which amino acids come out; fill in the codon table. | Mol | ★★★ |
| **Genes switch on and off** | François Jacob and Jacques Monod, early 1960s | Showed that a repressor turns the lac genes off until lactose is present. | The repressor letting go of DNA when lactose arrives. Same as the lac operon lab in MICRO.md. | C, Mol | ★★★ |
| **Recombinant DNA** | Stanley Cohen and Herbert Boyer, 1973 | Cut and pasted a gene into a plasmid and put it into bacteria. | Restriction enzymes cutting, ligase pasting, then transformation. Leads to the design-a-plasmid sim. | Mol | ★★ |
| **Frog cloning** | John Gurdon, 1962 | Put a nucleus from a tadpole's gut cell into an egg with its own nucleus destroyed. It grew into a frog. | Nuclear transfer under the microscope; the egg dividing into a tadpole. | M, C | ★★ |
| **Glowing protein** | Osamu Shimomura (1962), Martin Chalfie (1994) | Purified GFP from jellyfish; later made worms and bacteria glow by adding the gene. | The jellyfish, then GFP in other organisms. Our Ecoli lab already glows. | B, C, Mol | ★★ |
| **Reprogramming cells** | Shinya Yamanaka, 2006 | Four genes turned adult mouse skin cells into stem cells. | Cells changing identity; a "landscape" of cell fates. | C | ★★ |
| **CRISPR cuts DNA where you aim it** | Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuelle Charpentier, 2012 | Showed Cas9 plus a guide RNA cuts DNA at a chosen sequence. | Cas9 scanning DNA, the guide matching, the cut. | Mol | ★★★ |

## 4. Body and nerves

| Experiment | Who, when | On the bench | What the sim adds | Levels | Rating |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Blood circulates** | William Harvey, 1628 | Tied a band around an arm and pressed along veins to show valves let blood flow only toward the heart; calculated that the heart pumps far more than the body could make. | Do his arm demonstration on a model arm; valves opening and closing; his math as a calculator. | B, C | ★★ |
| **Animal electricity** | Luigi Galvani, 1780s | A frog's leg twitched when touched with two different metals. | The current reaching the nerve and muscle. (Show the setup with a model, not a real frog.) | B, C | ★★ |
| **Breathing is slow burning** | Antoine Lavoisier and Pierre-Simon Laplace, 1782–83 | Measured heat from a guinea pig by how much ice it melted, and compared with the heat from burning charcoal. | Ice melting around a chamber; then cells using O₂ to release energy. | B, C, Mol | ★★ |
| **The first hormone** | William Bayliss and Ernest Starling, 1902 | Found that acid in the gut releases a chemical (secretin) into the blood that tells the pancreas to secrete, even with the nerves cut. | A signal molecule riding the bloodstream to its target. | C, Mol | ★★ |
| **Nerves signal with chemicals** | Otto Loewi, 1921 | Said the idea came to him in a dream. Stimulated the nerve of one frog heart so it slowed, moved its fluid to a second heart, and the second heart slowed too. | Two beating hearts in dishes; the chemical (acetylcholine) moving from one to the other. Zoom into a synapse. | B, C, Mol | ★★★ |
| **Insulin** | Frederick Banting and Charles Best, 1921 | Extracted insulin from the pancreas and lowered blood sugar in diabetic animals, then in a patient. | Glucose and insulin at the cell; a blood-sugar graph. | C, Mol | ★★ |
| **How a nerve fires** | Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley, 1939–52 | Put electrodes inside a squid's giant axon and measured how sodium and potassium currents make the action potential. | Ion channels opening along the axon, the voltage trace on a scope. Students can block one channel type. | B, C, Mol | ★★★ |
| **The cell's power plant** | André Jagendorf and Ernest Uribe, 1966 | Soaked chloroplast membranes in acid, then moved them to a basic solution. They made ATP in the dark. | Protons rushing through ATP synthase as it spins; the proof that a pH difference alone drives it. | Mol | ★★★ |
| **Proteins fold themselves** | Christian Anfinsen, about 1961 | Unfolded the enzyme ribonuclease with chemicals; when they were removed, it refolded and worked again. | A chain collapsing back into its working shape. | Mol | ★★★ |
| **The organizer** | Hans Spemann and Hilde Mangold, 1924 | Grafted a small piece of one newt embryo into another. It organized a second body axis: twin embryos. | Graft the piece; watch the embryo develop a second body. | M, C | ★★ |

## 5. Evolution and ecology

| Experiment | Who, when | On the bench | What the sim adds | Levels | Rating |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Building blocks of life from a spark** | Stanley Miller and Harold Urey, 1952 | Sparked a sealed flask of water, methane, ammonia and hydrogen for a week. The water turned brown with amino acids. | The glass apparatus with sparks and the liquid darkening; molecules forming. | B, Mol | ★★★ |
| **Competition** | Georgy Gause, 1934 | Grew two Paramecium species alone and together. Together, one always drove the other out. | Swimming paramecia in a tube; population graphs. | B, M | ★★ |
| **Peppered moths** | Bernard Kettlewell, 1950s | Released and recaptured light and dark moths in sooty and clean woods. | Birds picking off the moths that stand out; generations shifting. | B | ★★ |
| **Keystone species** | Robert Paine, 1960s | Removed sea stars from a stretch of shore. Mussels took over and many other species disappeared. | A tide pool over years, with and without sea stars. | B | ★★ |
| **Guppy evolution** | John Endler, 1980 | Moved guppies between streams with and without predators. Their color patterns changed within a few generations. | Generations of guppies in two streams. | B | ★★ |
| **Darwin's finches** | Peter and Rosemary Grant, 1977 drought | During a drought only big, hard seeds were left. Finches with deeper beaks survived, and the next generation had deeper beaks. | Seeds, beaks and a population graph. | B | ★★ |
| **Long-term evolution** | Richard Lenski, 1988–present | 12 flasks of E. coli transferred every day for tens of thousands of generations. One line evolved to eat citrate. | Fast-forward the daily transfers; zoom in on the mutation that opened up a new food. | B, C, Mol | ★★★ |
| **The MEGA-plate** | Michael Baym, Roy Kishony and colleagues, 2016 | A giant plate with bands of rising antibiotic. Bacteria evolved resistance band by band in about 10 days. | Time-lapse of the front advancing; zoom into a resistant mutant. | B, M, C | ★★★ |

## 7. How to present them

- **Predict first.** Most of these had a surprising answer. Ask students what they expect before
  running it (van Helmont: where did the mass come from?).
- **Show the competing ideas.** Meselson–Stahl tested three models of copying. Show each one's
  predicted bands next to the real result.
- **The scientist's view, then ours.** Run the experiment as the scientist saw it, then zoom in to
  show what was really going on, including where they were wrong (van Helmont's water).
- **Original data.** Where the numbers are known (van Helmont's weights, Mendel's counts), let students
  check them.
- **Animals.** Several classics used animals (Priestley's mice, Griffith's mice, Galvani's and Loewi's
  frogs). Show them with models or abstract readouts rather than as realistic animals.

## 8. Good first picks

The picks that made the launch and first-year lists are in [ROADMAP.md](ROADMAP.md).

Chosen for a strong visual story, for reusing what we have or plan to build, and for a clear "aha".

1. **The mass of a tree (van Helmont):** simple bench, a fast-forward, and a molecule twist. A good
   test of time-lapse state.
2. **Engelmann's spectrum:** a microscope view with a rainbow and swimming bacteria. Uses the eyepiece
   view and swimmers from MICRO.md.
3. **Meselson–Stahl:** a centrifuge tube and colored DNA strands. Uses the DNA kit.
4. **Hershey–Chase:** phages, a blender and a centrifuge. Uses the cell and DNA kits.
5. **Pasteur's swan-neck flask:** one beautiful prop and a tilt interaction.
6. **Loewi's two hearts:** a dramatic story (the dream), with a synapse zoom.
